DFU Vision · technical walk-through

Two networks, and the crop between them

What actually happens to a photograph between the shutter and the Texas code on screen, with the tensor shapes and the reason the pipeline is shaped this way.

Segmentation: 5,933,177 parameters Classification: 2,263,108 parameters Both run in the browser

The short version: a photograph is passed through a segmentation network that outlines the ulcer, the outline is turned into a square crop, and that crop is passed through a classification network with two outputs. Nothing about the wound's position is inferred by the classifier. That division of labour is the whole design, and it exists because the earlier single-network version could not find the wound.

Stage 1

Segmentation: find the wound

224×224×3  →  224×224×1

The photograph is centre-cropped to a square and resized to 224 by 224 pixels, then each channel is rescaled from the 0–255 range to −1 to +1, which is what the ImageNet-pretrained weights expect.

The network is a U-Net built on a MobileNetV2 encoder. The encoder halves the resolution five times, from 224 down to 7 pixels, while deepening the representation from 3 channels to 1,280. That gives it semantic understanding — "this texture is granulation tissue" — but it has thrown away the precise location. The decoder climbs back up, and at each step it concatenates the matching-resolution tensor from the encoder before convolving. Those skip connections are what restore spatial precision: the deep layers say what a region is, the shallow ones say exactly where its edge falls.

How it was trained

The target is the expert-drawn wound mask from the public FUSeg 2021 dataset: 787 usable training images and 190 for validation. The loss adds Dice to ordinary binary cross-entropy. That matters because the wound is about 2 per cent of the pixels, so a network that predicted "background everywhere" would already be 98 per cent accurate by cross-entropy alone. Dice measures overlap between the predicted and true regions, so it is not fooled by that.

Augmentation applies the same rotations and flips to the image and the mask together, while brightness, contrast and saturation jitter apply to the image only. Getting that wrong — rotating the photograph but not its mask — is the classic way to train a segmentation model on nonsense.

Stage 2

Post-processing: one wound, not forty

224×224×1 probabilities  →  one bounding box

The network outputs a probability per pixel, not a decision. Three steps turn it into a usable region:

  1. Threshold at 0.5. Every pixel above it becomes wound.
  2. Keep the largest connected region. A flood fill over four-connected neighbours labels each separate blob, and all but the biggest are discarded. Without this step, speckle on a drape or a glove joins the mask and the bounding box stretches across the whole photograph — which defeats the entire point of cropping.
  3. Take the bounding box of what survives, and record the pixel count and mean probability for the on-screen area and confidence figures.
Stage 3

The crop: the step that makes it work

bounding box  →  square × 2.2  →  224×224×3

The bounding box is squared off around its centre, then expanded by 60 per cent on each side, giving a box 2.2 times the wound's longest dimension. It is clamped to stay inside the photograph, and the region is resampled from the full-resolution image rather than from the 224-pixel copy, so the crop gains real detail rather than upscaled blur.

The margin is deliberate. A crop tight to the wound edge would discard the peri-wound skin, and erythema spreading beyond the wound margin is a genuine sign of infection. The 60 per cent figure keeps that ring in frame.

Why this is the hinge

In the FUSeg validation images the ulcer occupies a median 1.3 per cent of the photograph. After cropping it occupies about 9 per cent. Retraining the classifier on those crops raised severity accuracy from 84.1 to 92.5 per cent and infection accuracy from 75.2 to 81.5 per cent. The architecture of the classifier did not change; only what it was shown.

Stage 4

Classification: two questions, one backbone

224×224×3  →  7×7×1280  →  1280  →  2 × softmax

MobileNetV2 again, ImageNet weights again, and again the same two-phase recipe: train the new output layers with the backbone frozen, then unfreeze its top 30 convolutional layers and fine-tune at a learning rate fifty times lower. Batch-normalisation layers stay frozen throughout, because their running statistics were estimated on a million ImageNet images and a few hundred wound photographs would only add noise to them.

The backbone ends at a 7 by 7 grid of 1,280 numbers. Global average pooling collapses each of those 1,280 channels to a single value, discarding position entirely and leaving a 1,280-number description of the crop. Two independent output layers read that same description:

  • Severity head — mild, meaning a superficial Texas Grade 1 appearance, against severe, meaning deep or extensive, a Grade 3 appearance.
  • Infection head — no infection against infection suspected, which maps onto Texas Stage A and Stage B.

Sharing the backbone between the two heads is the point of transfer learning: the features that describe a wound bed are useful for both questions, and only 5,124 of the model's 2.26 million parameters belong to the heads themselves. Each head is weighted so the minority class is not ignored, and images whose weak label is ambiguous contribute zero weight to that head's loss while still training the other.

Ischaemia is not predicted, it is entered. Perfusion cannot be read off a photograph, and Texas stages C and D exist precisely to record it, so the app asks for pedal pulses, ABPI or toe pressure and refuses to guess. Where a model trained on Wagner-named folders is loaded instead, the Wagner grade comes straight from that model rather than from this mapping.

Grad-CAM, and why it is a side branch

Grad-CAM asks how much each region of the input contributed to one chosen class score. The computation, done live in the browser, is four steps:

  1. Run the crop forward and keep the 7×7×1280 activations from the last convolutional layer.
  2. Take the score for the selected class and compute its gradient with respect to those activations.
  3. Average each channel's gradient over the 7 by 7 grid, giving 1,280 importance weights, then form the weighted sum of the activation channels and clip the negatives away.
  4. Normalise the resulting 7 by 7 map to 0–1 and resize it up for display.

The colour is therefore a measure of the model's attention. Red means "this area counted most towards the class in the dropdown". It is not a clinical colour: it does not mean infection, bleeding or erythema, and a red patch on healthy skin is a warning about the model rather than an observation about the foot.

Its resolution is fixed by that 7 by 7 grid, and this is the whole problem.

What runs where

Both models are converted to TensorFlow.js at half precision and execute in the browser on WebGL, so no photograph is uploaded anywhere. The trade is size and speed: about 21 MB on first load, cached afterwards, and roughly two and a half to four seconds for a full pass in a software renderer. A real phone GPU is quicker.

Inside the app, Grad-CAM needs the network split in two — one model from the input to the 7×7×1280 layer, another from there to the outputs — so the activations can be held and differentiated. The heatmap is drawn only inside the crop rectangle it actually explains, because painting it over the whole photograph would put the heat in the wrong place.

The caveat that governs every number here

The segmentation targets are genuine expert masks. The classification labels are not: they are weak labels derived by rule from wound area and wound-bed colour, because the public dataset carries no Texas grades and no culture results. Every classifier figure on this page therefore describes agreement with a proxy, not clinical accuracy. Replacing those proxies with adjudicated grades and culture-confirmed infection status is what the ground-truth form in the app exists to do.
