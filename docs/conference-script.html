Conference talk · presenter's script

Teaching a phone to look at a diabetic foot

Fifteen slides and three technical backup slides. The clinical case is in plain language for an audience with no background in computing; the method, the terminology and the pipeline are specified in full.

Dr Naim Bin Abdul Malek Hospital Tengku Permaisuri Norashikin 15 slides + 3 backup · ~17 min, or ~23 with the method read aloud

How to use this script

Each slide has two registers, and they are for different moments. What you say is plain language, written to be read more or less verbatim to a clinical audience. Method is the same step stated properly — architecture, terminology, hyperparameters, measured figures.

Read the plain script aloud and let the method panels sit unread; they answer questions, and Backup A to C are three slides you can put up when one is asked. That is the seventeen-minute talk. If the room turns out to be technical, read the method panels in place of the plain paragraphs for slides 6, 7, 8, 12 and 13 — they are written to be spoken — which makes it about twenty-three minutes.

Slides marked with a maroon bar are the core. Cut to ten minutes by keeping those and dropping the rest. Do not cut into the pipeline, Part 2; it is the spine of the talk. Green highlights mark the numbers worth landing slowly.

Read the whole thing aloud once with a timer. Talks always run long, and the pipeline is where you will want the spare minute.

Part 1 · Why anyone should care

Title

What you say

Good morning. I am going to show you something my phone can do that it could not do a year ago. It can look at a photograph of a diabetic foot ulcer and tell you two things: how deep the wound looks, and whether it looks infected. Slide 290 sec

Why a foot ulcer is a life-or-death problem

On the slide
Four numbers, large, one per quadrant. No paragraphs.
  • 15.6 % of Malaysian adults have diabetes
  • 1 in 3 will develop a foot ulcer
  • 30.5 % die within five years of getting one
  • 56.6 % die within five years of a major amputation
What you say

Let me start with why this matters, because the numbers are worse than most people assume.

In Malaysia, the proportion of adults with diabetes rose from eleven per cent in 2011 to fifteen point six per cent in 2023. A substantial share of them will develop a foot ulcer at some point.

Here is the part that surprises clinicians outside this field. Once a patient has a diabetic foot ulcer, their chance of dying within five years is thirty per cent. If they lose part of the limb, it is fifty-seven per cent. That is worse than many cancers we treat far more aggressively.

And in our own setting, roughly eighteen per cent of Malaysian patients with a diabetic foot ulcer eventually undergo an amputation.

Slide 360 sec

Infection is the thing that decides

On the slide
A simple two-arrow diagram: ulcer → infected → amputation, and ulcer → not infected → healing. One caption: "more than half become infected".
What you say

What turns an ulcer into an amputation is usually infection. More than half of all foot ulcers become infected, and seventeen per cent of infected ulcers end in amputation.

So the single most valuable thing you can do early is recognise infection and refer. That is a decision made in a clinic, often by a medical officer who sees a foot ulcer a few times a month, not by a specialist who sees them daily.

That gap is what I have been working on.

Part 2 · The pipeline, from photograph to answer

Slide 445 sec

What we built, in one sentence

On the slide
A phone held in a hand, showing the app with a wound outlined. One line of text: "A web page that runs on any phone. Nothing is uploaded."
What you say

It is a web page. You open it on a phone browser, photograph the wound, and it answers. There is no app to install and no account to create.

The important detail is this: the photograph never leaves the phone. All the computing happens inside the browser, on the handset in your pocket. That matters for patient privacy, and it means it works when the clinic's internet does not.

Method — what is actually running
  • Client-side inference. Three TensorFlow.js LayersModel graphs execute in the browser on the WebGL backend. There is no server round trip and no API call, so there is nothing to intercept and nothing to log.
  • Payload. Segmentation model 12 MB, wound-crop classifier 4.5 MB, whole-frame classifier 4.5 MB. All three are float16-quantised — half-precision weights, which halves the download for a loss in accuracy too small to measure here. About 21 MB on first load, cached by the browser thereafter.
  • Latency. A full pass — segment, crop, classify four augmented views, Grad-CAM — takes 2.5 to 4 seconds in a software renderer, and less on a phone GPU.
  • Architecture of the whole thing. A single static HTML page. No build step, no framework, no database, no backend. That is a deliberate constraint: it is auditable by one person, and it cannot silently start uploading.
Slide 545 sec

Step 1 — take the photograph properly

On the slide
The six-point acquisition protocol, with a good photo and a bad photo side by side.
What you say

Step one has nothing to do with computers. Before the app does anything, it makes you agree to a photography protocol. Dressing off, wound irrigated and blotted dry. Camera thirty to forty centimetres away, straight on. Flash off. Plain sheet behind the foot. A paper ruler beside the wound, never on it. And no faces or name bands in the frame.

I put this first deliberately, because in wound photography the camera is the biggest source of error, not the computer. Two photographs of the same ulcer taken at different distances and under different lights can look like different wounds. If we do not standardise this, nothing downstream can be trusted.

If you have timeHold up your own phone and mime the distance. The audience remembers gestures better than bullet points.
Method — why acquisition is a modelling problem
  • Input tensor. 224 × 224 × 3, rescaled to the range [-1, 1] by x / 127.5 - 1. That is MobileNetV2's own preprocess_input. The browser performs the identical arithmetic in JavaScript, so training-time and inference-time preprocessing cannot drift apart — a common and silent source of degraded performance in deployed models.
  • What the protocol controls. Distance, illumination, white balance and background are unmodelled covariates. Varying them moves the input away from the distribution the model was fitted on — covariate shift. Data augmentation makes a model tolerant of variation it has seen; it does not make it tolerant of variation nobody standardised. Controlling variance at the point of capture is cheaper and more honest than correcting it afterwards.
  • Scale. The paper ruler is a fiducial marker. Two taps on it give a pixels-per-millimetre factor, which is what converts a pixel count into square centimetres. Without it the area is reported in pixels and is not comparable between visits.
Slide 675 sec

Step 2 — the computer traces round the wound

On the slide
One photograph, before and after, with the teal outline drawn. Two numbers beneath: "agreement with experts 0.85" and "centre lands inside the real wound in 89 % of photographs".
What you say

The first thing the computer does is draw round the wound edge. The proper name for this is semantic segmentation — deciding, for every single pixel, whether it is wound or not wound. I will call it drawing round the wound.

If you have ever traced a wound onto a transparency sheet to measure how it is healing, this is exactly the same idea, done automatically in about a second.

We taught it using roughly a thousand photographs where wound specialists had already drawn the outline by hand. It now agrees closely with those experts. The overlap score is zero point eight five, where one point zero would be a perfect match. And the centre of its outline falls inside the real wound in eighty-nine out of a hundred photographs.

Because it has the outline, it can also measure the wound. Tap the two ends of the paper ruler, tell it how long that is, and it reports the area in square centimetres. That gives you a number to compare at the next visit, instead of "looks a bit smaller".

Method — the segmentation model in full
  • Task. Semantic segmentation: classify every pixel as wound or not wound, producing a mask rather than a label.
  • Architecture. MobileNetV2-UNet. The encoder is MobileNetV2 (α = 1.0, ImageNet weights) which compresses the image to a 7×7 representation. The decoder is four upsample → concatenate → convolve blocks of 192, 96, 48 and 24 channels that expand it back out. Each block concatenates the encoder tensor of matching resolution — block_13_expand_relu at 14², block_6_expand_relu at 28², block_3_expand_relu at 56², block_1_expand_relu at 112². Those cross-links are the skip connections, and they are the whole point of a U-Net: the deep layers know what the thing is, the shallow layers still know exactly where its edge was, and the skips let the decoder use both. The output is a single-channel sigmoid mask — one wound probability per pixel.
  • Loss. Soft Dice plus binary cross-entropy. Cross-entropy alone is dominated by the background, which is most of the image; the Dice term scores overlap with the true mask directly, so a small wound cannot be ignored cheaply.
  • Training. Two phases, Adam, batch 16. Phase 1: encoder frozen, learning rate 1e-3, up to 12 epochs, early stopping on validation Dice. Phase 2: the top of the encoder unfrozen at 5e-5 for 6 epochs, with all BatchNormalization layers held frozen so their running statistics are not corrupted by small batches. Total 17.6 minutes.
  • Data. FUSeg 2021 (AZH Wound and Vascular Center) — 787 training and 190 held-out images, each with a mask drawn by a wound specialist.
  • Held-out performance. Dice 0.848 mean, 0.894 median; IoU 0.760; predicted centroid inside the true ulcer in 88.9 % of images.
  • Post-processing. In the browser: threshold at 0.5, then 4-connected component labelling (a flood fill) keeping only the largest region. This discards speckle, so one stray positive pixel on a glove cannot drag the region of interest off the wound.
Slide 760 sec

Step 3 — it steps closer

On the slide
The same photo with a dashed square around the wound, then the cropped close-up beside it. Caption: "1.3 % of the photo → 9 %". Below: "accuracy 84 % → 92 %".
What you say

This step is called cropping to the region of interest. It sounds trivial and it is the most important thing in the whole design.

In our photographs, a typical ulcer takes up only one point three per cent of the picture. The rest is foot, bed sheet, gloves, and the clinic floor. Asking a computer to judge infection from that is like asking you to assess a wound from a photograph of the whole ward.

So once it knows where the wound is, it crops in around it, keeping a margin of surrounding skin because redness spreading beyond the wound edge is itself a sign of infection. The wound now fills about nine per cent of what the computer sees.

That one change lifted accuracy from eighty-four per cent to ninety-two per cent. We did not build a cleverer computer. We just stood it closer.

Method — the region of interest, and why it matters this much
  • The geometry. At a 224 × 224 input, an object occupying 1.3 % of the frame is about 25 pixels across. MobileNetV2 has five stride-2 stages, so the final convolutional map is 7 × 7: each cell summarises a 32-pixel square. The ulcer is therefore smaller than one cell of the representation the classifier reasons over. This is the standard small-object problem in a fixed-input convolutional network, and no change of architecture at that input size fixes it.
  • The crop. Take the bounding box of the retained mask component, square it, dilate it by a 60 % margin on each side (2.2× linear) and clamp to the image. The margin is clinical, not cosmetic: erythema spreading beyond the wound edge is itself a sign of infection, so the model must be allowed to see peri-wound skin.
  • Matched training. A second classifier was trained on crops produced by this same geometry — 717 training and 171 validation crops — so the distribution at training matches the distribution at inference. Cropping at test time only would have been a train–test mismatch.
  • Measured effect. Median wound occupancy rises from 1.3 % to 9.0 % of the input. Whole frame → crop: severity AUC 0.927 → 0.963, accuracy 84.1 → 92.5 %; infection AUC 0.805 → 0.834, accuracy 75.2 → 81.5 %. Same architecture, same data, same weights initialisation — the gain is entirely resolution recovered.
Slide 860 sec

Step 4 — it answers two questions

On the slide
Two gauges: "How deep does it look?" and "Does it look infected?", each with a percentage. Nothing else.
What you say

Now it looks at the close-up and answers two separate questions. In the jargon these are two classification heads sharing one set of eyes — two questions answered from the same look at the wound.

First, how deep does this look: superficial, or deep and extensive. Second, does this look infected.

Each answer comes with a percentage, and I want to be precise about what that percentage means. It is the computer's confidence in that one label. It is not a severity score. Eighty per cent does not mean a worse wound than sixty per cent; it means the computer is more sure of its answer.

And when it is not sure, it says so. Below seventy per cent confidence it refuses to answer and tells you to refer. I will come back to why that refusal matters.

Method — the classifier, and the three things bolted onto it
  • Architecture. One MobileNetV2 backbone (α = 1.0, ImageNet weights). Global average pooling over out_relu, the final 7×7×1280 feature map, then dropout 0.3, then two independent softmax heads, each Dense(2): grade (mild / severe) and infection (none / suspected). This is multi-task learning on a shared representation — both questions read the same features, which is efficient and appropriate when the underlying visual cues overlap.
  • Class imbalance. Per-head sample weights up-weight the minority class so the loss is balanced. Cases whose label is ambiguous for one head are given weight zero for that head only, so they still train the other. This is why the two heads have different effective sample sizes.
  • Test-time augmentation. Four views — identity, horizontal flip, vertical flip, both — with the probabilities averaged. A wound has no canonical orientation, so this is a free variance reduction: infection AUC 0.834 → 0.863, severity 0.963 → 0.965.
  • Calibration. Platt scaling: p_cal = σ(a · logit(p) + b), a one-parameter-per-term rescaling of confidence that leaves the ranking of cases untouched. Applied to the severity head (a = 0.755, b = -0.302), which lifts the calibration slope from 0.78 to 0.97 at no cost in accuracy. Not applied to infection: the four-view averaging already brings that slope to 0.96, and over 20 disjoint half-splits Platt scaling gave no Brier improvement and cost accuracy (0.835 → 0.809). Both coefficient sets are recorded in calibration.json with the reason, so the decision is auditable.
  • Selective prediction. If the maximum posterior is below 0.70 the head abstains. On the infection head that retains 66 % of cases at 89 % accuracy, against 83 % if it is forced to answer everything. Trading coverage for reliability is the correct direction for a triage tool: a declined case costs a referral, a confident wrong answer costs a foot.
  • What the percentage is. A posterior probability for one label, not a severity score and not a risk. Eighty per cent for “severe” does not mean a worse wound than sixty; it means less doubt about the word.
Slide 960 sec

Step 5 — the clinician supplies what a photograph cannot

On the slide
The Texas grid with one cell highlighted, and a blue box off to the side labelled "you: pulses, ABPI, toe pressure".
What you say

The two answers then map onto the classification systems you already use. The app shows both the University of Texas grid and the Wagner grades.

But notice what it does not do. The Texas system also asks whether the foot is ischaemic. No photograph can show blood supply. So the app asks you. You enter whether the pulses are present, or the ABPI, or the toe pressure, and only then does it complete the classification.

I think this is the right instinct for clinical AI generally. The tool should do the part it can genuinely do, and ask you for the part it cannot, rather than guessing to look clever.

Method — bounding the output space to the input modality
  • The formal problem. The Texas stage requires perfusion, and perfusion is not merely unlabelled in our data — it is not identifiable from an RGB photograph at all. No quantity of training data recovers a variable the sensor cannot observe. Attempting it would produce a model that appears to predict ischaemia while in fact predicting some correlate of it, such as skin tone or lighting.
  • The design response. Restrict the model to what the modality supports — depth and infection appearance — and obtain the rest by structured elicitation from the clinician: pulses present or absent, ABPI, or toe pressure. The Texas cell is then composed: cell = grade × (infection stage + clinician ischaemia).
  • Why Wagner is offered as well. Wagner grade 3 and above turn on probe-to-bone and radiology, and Wagner 4–5 on gangrene extent. Those are examination and imaging findings, so the app takes them as input rather than predicting them. Offering both systems also lets a clinician record in whichever they already use, which matters for whether the case log is ever filled in.
Slide 1060 sec

Step 6 — the loop that makes it better

On the slide
A circle: photograph → AI answer → clinician's own grade and the culture result → better training data → back to the start.
What you say

The last step is the one that turns a demonstration into a research tool.

After the app gives its answer, the clinician records the truth: their own grading, whether the probe reached bone, and when it comes back, the tissue culture and sensitivity result. The app keeps a log of AI answer against real answer, and can export it as a spreadsheet.

So every patient seen makes the next version better. And it means we can state plainly how often the tool agrees with a culture result, rather than how often it agrees with somebody's impression.

Method — from weak labels to a reference standard
  • What the log holds. Each case stores the model's posteriors for both heads, the clinician's own grade and stage, probe-to-bone, and the tissue culture and sensitivity organisms when they return, with a CSV export.
  • Why that is the whole point. Every figure in this talk is measured against weak labels — a visual proxy. The log is the mechanism that replaces them with a clinician-adjudicated, culture-confirmed reference standard. Until that exists, nothing here is a diagnostic accuracy estimate; it is an engineering check.
  • What it unlocks. Refitting the calibration and the abstention threshold on real labels rather than proxies; estimating sensitivity and specificity against culture; and reporting under TRIPOD+AI and STARD instead of quoting internal validation numbers.
  • Governance first. No patient image is loaded until NMRR registration and MREC approval are in place, with de-identification and consent under the PDPA 2010. The readiness dashboard in the app counts images per class against a target of 100 and states plainly when a class is too thin to train on.

Part 3 · How it learned, and where it fails

Slide 1275 sec

We did not teach it from nothing

On the slide
Two panels. Left: a grid of everyday photographs (cats, cars, chairs) labelled "already learned to see, 1.4 million pictures". Right: foot ulcers labelled "then learned wounds, about 800 pictures".
What you say

People imagine that building this needs a supercomputer and a million wound photographs. It does not, because of one idea.

We started with a program that had already looked at one point four million everyday photographs — cats, cars, chairs. From those it had already learned the general business of seeing: edges, colours, textures, shapes.

We then showed it about eight hundred foot ulcer photographs and let it adjust what it already knew. It is the difference between hiring an experienced photographer and teaching them wound photography, versus teaching a newborn to see.

That is why this took about five minutes of training on an ordinary laptop, and why a district hospital can do this. The specialists call it transfer learning. You can call it standing on someone else's shoulders.

Method — transfer learning, stated properly
  • What is transferred. MobileNetV2 pretrained on ImageNet-1k — 1.28 million photographs, 1,000 everyday classes. We keep the convolutional feature extractor and discard its classifier. Early layers encode edges, colour opponency and texture; middle layers encode parts and shapes. Those are generic to photographs, wounds included, which is why they need not be relearned from 800 images.
  • Two-phase schedule. Phase 1 — backbone frozen, heads only, Adam at 1e-3, 10 epochs. The randomly initialised heads produce large early gradients; freezing the backbone means those gradients cannot destroy the pretrained weights. Phase 2 — fine-tune the top 30 layers only, Adam at 2e-5, fifty times lower, for 4 epochs, with BatchNormalization layers kept frozen. The low rate and the shallow depth of unfreezing are what stop catastrophic forgetting on a small dataset.
  • Why MobileNetV2 and not something larger. 3.5 million parameters, depthwise-separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks: roughly a ninth of the multiply-accumulate operations of a standard convolution at similar accuracy. That is what makes a 4.5 MB browser download and phone-side inference possible at all. With around 800 images, a ResNet-50 or a vision transformer would overfit sooner and could not be deployed this way. The constraint chose the architecture.
  • Cost and reproducibility. About five minutes on an ordinary laptop CPU for the classifier, 17.6 minutes for the segmentation model. Seeds fixed at 0; the training scripts and the audit scripts are in the repository, so every number in this talk can be recomputed.
Slide 132 min

The thing that surprised me

On the slide
One large picture: a 7 × 7 grid over a photograph, with one square shaded and a tiny ulcer inside it. Two labels: "one square = 2 % of the picture" and "a typical ulcer = 1.3 %".
What you say

If you remember one thing from this talk, let it be this slide.

There is a very popular technique for explaining what these systems are doing. Its proper name is Grad-CAM, a saliency map; everyone calls it a heat map. It colours the picture to show which parts the computer leaned on. Every paper shows one. It looks convincing. I had it in my own first version, and I presented it as our explainability feature.

Then we measured whether it actually points at the ulcer. It does not. The hottest spot landed inside the real ulcer in only thirteen out of a hundred photographs.

The reason is simple once you see this picture. The heat map can only see the photograph as a grid of forty-nine squares. Each square covers about two per cent of the image. A typical ulcer in our data covers one point three per cent. The wound is smaller than the smallest thing the heat map is able to see. It was never going to work, and no amount of tuning would have fixed it.

So we stopped using it to locate the wound and used the tracing instead, which lands correctly eighty-nine per cent of the time. The heat map is still in the app, but labelled honestly: it tells you whether the computer is looking at the foot or at the curtains. Nothing more.

The general lesson is not about foot ulcers. If somebody shows you a heat map as evidence that their AI is trustworthy, ask them whether they measured it.

Method — Grad-CAM, and how the failure was measured
  • What Grad-CAM computes. Take the final convolutional map (out_relu, 7×7×1280). Compute the gradient of the target class score with respect to each channel of that map, and global-average-pool those gradients to get one weight per channel — how much this channel mattered to this prediction. Form the weighted sum of the channels, apply ReLU to keep only positive evidence, normalise, and bilinearly upsample to the input size. That last step is where the trouble hides.
  • The audit. The pointing game, on the 190 held-out images that carry expert masks: does the heat map's peak pixel fall inside the true wound? Also the fraction of CAM mass inside the mask, divided by the mask's area fraction, which gives performance relative to chance.
  • Results. Whole frame: peak inside the wound 13 %, mass 2.8× chance. Cropped to the wound: 47 %, 3.1×. Retrained on crops: 21 %, 1.8×.
  • Why it cannot be tuned away. 224 / 7 = 32 pixels per cell, so one cell is 2.04 % of the frame and the median ulcer is 1.3 %. The unit of explanation is coarser than the object being explained. Bilinear upsampling then produces a smooth, precise-looking image whose underlying representation contains no such precision — the map looks like evidence and is an artefact of interpolation.
  • The honest reading of the retrained case. Its pointing score is worse, and that may be correct behaviour: trained on crops, the model legitimately attends to peri-wound skin, where erythema is a genuine infection sign. A saliency map cannot distinguish “looking in the wrong place” from “looking at a different right place”. That is precisely why saliency is not a validation instrument, and it is consistent with the published literature on saliency reliability in medical imaging.
  • What replaced it. Segmentation, whose centroid lands inside the true ulcer in 88.9 % of the same images. Grad-CAM is retained, drawn only inside the crop it explains, and labelled on screen as a check on whether the model is reading the wound or the room.
Slide 1490 sec

What it gets wrong

On the slide
Three short headings only: "Our labels are guesses", "It misses sloughy edges", "It has never seen a Malaysian foot". Beside the second, show the under-traced example.
What you say

Three weaknesses, stated plainly, because a tool whose limits you cannot describe is a tool nobody should use.

First, our labels are guesses. The technical term is weak labels. The public photographs we trained on came with no culture results and no clinical grades. So we inferred infection from visual clues: how much yellow slough, how much black tissue. That is a proxy, not a diagnosis. Every accuracy figure I have quoted measures agreement with our guesses, not with the truth.

Second, it under-traces sloughy wounds. On one of my own examples it outlined the top third and missed the yellow slough below it, reporting about a third of the true area. The average is good; individual cases are not always.

Third, it has never seen a Malaysian foot. Every photograph came from clinics in the United States and the United Kingdom. Different skin tones, different cameras, different lighting. There is no reason to assume the numbers I have shown you will hold in a Klinik Kesihatan, and good reason to assume they will drop.

Why this slide wins the roomMost AI talks skip it. Presenting your failures with measurements makes the rest of your numbers believable, and it is the slide senior clinicians will remember you for.
Method — the limitations in technical terms
  • Weak supervision. The infection label is a rule over mask-derived colour features in HSV space: suspected if the slough fraction exceeds 0.10 or the necrosis fraction exceeds 0.15; none if slough is below 0.01 and necrosis below 0.02; otherwise the case is excluded from that head rather than guessed. A peri-wound erythema term was computed and deliberately dropped — healthy granulation tissue is also red, and including it lowered infection AUC from 0.805 to 0.72. So every accuracy figure quoted is agreement with a proxy, and an upper bound on nothing clinical.
  • Split granularity. The train / validation split is image-level, not patient-level, because FUSeg publishes no patient identifiers. Two images of the same ulcer may therefore straddle the split, which biases the estimates optimistically. The prospective study will split by patient.
  • Domain shift. All data comes from centres in the United States and United Kingdom. Skin tone distribution is unreported in the source datasets, and Malaysian cameras, lighting and case mix differ. Expect the numbers to fall on first external testing; a model that does not fall is usually a model that has leaked.
  • Spectrum bias. The training images are hospital wound-clinic cases. The intended deployment is primary care, where the prevalence of severe and infected ulcers is lower. The same model, unchanged, will have a lower positive predictive value there — a property of prevalence, not of the weights.
  • What has not been done. No external validation, no prospective evaluation, no patient outcome measured, no regulatory assessment, no DECIDE-AI style evaluation of clinician interaction. Under TRIPOD+AI this is internal development and internal validation only, and it should be described as such.
Slide 1575 sec

What it is not, and what comes next

On the slide
Two columns. "Not yet": not a medical device, not validated, not for treatment decisions. "Next": 400 patients, culture-confirmed labels, ethics submitted, primary care validation.
What you say

Let me be clear about what this is not. It is a research prototype. It is not a medical device, it is not validated, and nobody should make a treatment decision with it. The app says so on its own opening screen and will not let you past until you agree.

What comes next is the part that actually matters. I am registering a study to collect around four hundred patients with proper consent, where each ulcer is graded independently by two wound clinicians, disagreements are settled by a consultant, and infection is confirmed by tissue culture rather than by how it looks.

Then the question stops being "can a computer do this" and becomes "does it help a medical officer in Kajang refer the right patient sooner". That is a harder question and a more useful one.

Every metric, defined

On the slide
This table. Use it if somebody challenges a number, so that you are defining the measure rather than defending the score.
Measures used in this work
  • Dice coefficient. Twice the overlap between the predicted and true mask, divided by their combined area. 1.0 is identical, 0 is no overlap. Ours: 0.848 mean, 0.894 median. For wound segmentation, 0.8 and above is the range published methods report on this dataset.
  • Intersection over union. The same idea, scored more harshly — overlap divided by combined extent. Always lower than Dice. Ours: 0.760.
  • Centroid-in-wound. Does the centre of the predicted outline fall inside the true wound? A blunt but decision-relevant check, because it is what determines whether the crop lands on the ulcer. Ours: 88.9 %.
  • AUC (area under the ROC curve). The probability that a randomly chosen positive case is ranked above a randomly chosen negative one. 0.5 is chance. It measures discrimination — the ordering of cases — and says nothing about whether the probabilities themselves are trustworthy. Ours: severity 0.963, infection 0.863 with augmentation.
  • Accuracy. The proportion of cases labelled correctly at the chosen threshold. Sensitive to class balance, which is why it is quoted alongside AUC and not instead of it. Ours: severity 92.5 %, infection 81.5 %.
  • Calibration slope. Regress outcome on the predicted log-odds: 1.0 means the stated confidence matches observed frequency, below 1.0 means the model is overconfident. Ours: severity 0.78 raw, 0.97 after Platt scaling; infection 0.96 after augmentation. This is the measure that decides whether “eighty per cent” may be shown to a clinician at all.
  • Brier score. Mean squared error of the predicted probabilities — a single number combining discrimination and calibration. Lower is better. Used here to check that calibration was not bought at the cost of something else.
  • Pointing game. The localisation test for saliency maps: does the hottest pixel fall inside the true object? Grad-CAM: 13 %. Segmentation centroid: 88.9 %.
  • Coverage and accuracy-on-retained. The selective-prediction pair. At a 0.70 threshold the infection head answers 66 % of cases at 89 % accuracy, against 83 % if forced to answer all. Report both numbers or neither; quoting the retained accuracy alone is how abstention gets abused.

What the study needs to be

On the slide
Three lines: "400 patients", "two independent raters, consultant adjudication", "culture-confirmed infection". Then: "binary referral endpoint, not six Wagner grades".
Design of the prospective phase
  • Endpoint. A binary referral decision — does this ulcer need same-week specialist review — not a six-class Wagner prediction. The arithmetic forces this: to estimate performance for Wagner 5 with any precision you need around 50 such cases, and at their prevalence that means recruiting on the order of 2,500 patients. A binary endpoint is answerable at 400 and is also the decision a GP actually makes.
  • Reference standard. Two wound clinicians grade independently, disagreements adjudicated by a consultant; infection confirmed by tissue culture and sensitivity, not by appearance. Inter-rater agreement reported as κ, because a reference standard without a reliability figure is not a reference standard.
  • Sample size. Around 400 patients, sized on the standard error of an AUC (Hanley–McNeil) and on the precision of a proportion, which gives a usable confidence interval for sensitivity at the referral threshold.
  • Splitting. By patient, not by image, so that no ulcer contributes to both development and testing.
  • Governance. NMRR registration, then MREC approval — realistically two months or more — with de-identification and consent under the PDPA 2010. Reporting to TRIPOD+AI and STARD, with CLAIM for the imaging description and DECIDE-AI when clinician interaction is evaluated.

Backup · questions you should expect

Is this going to replace clinicians?

No, and the design says so. It refuses to answer when it is unsure, and it asks the clinician for the vascular assessment it cannot do. It is a second opinion for someone who sees a foot ulcer twice a month, not a substitute for someone who sees them daily.

How accurate is it, really?

On our held-out photographs, about ninety-two per cent for depth and eighty-two per cent for infection. But those are measured against our own visual guesses, not against culture results, so treat them as an engineering check rather than clinical accuracy. That is exactly what the next study is for.

What about patient confidentiality?

The photograph never leaves the phone. All the computing happens in the browser. Nothing is uploaded to any server. And no patient image goes anywhere near it until ethics approval is in place.

How much did this cost?

Nothing so far but time. The photographs are public research datasets, the training ran on a free graphics card in a web browser, and the app is hosted free. That is the point worth making to a ministry: the barrier here is data and governance, not money.

Why not just use one of the commercial wound apps?

Several exist and they are good at measuring wounds. None of them classify infection, which is the decision that drives amputation. And none were built or validated on Malaysian patients.

Could it work for pressure ulcers or venous ulcers?

Probably, with retraining, because the underlying method is not specific to feet. But I would not claim it until it had been tested, and the grading systems are different.

What happens if somebody photographs the wrong thing?

We tested that. Point it at a desk and it finds no wound, both answers fall below the confidence threshold, and it declines to classify and advises referral. It used to produce a confident answer for a photograph of a desk. Fixing that was one of the more useful afternoons of the project.

Why MobileNetV2 and not a larger, more accurate model?

Because the deployment constraint came first. The model has to download over clinic internet and run inside a phone browser, which puts a hard ceiling of a few megabytes and a few hundred million operations. MobileNetV2 meets it at 3.5 million parameters using depthwise-separable convolutions. And with roughly 800 training images, a ResNet-50 or a vision transformer would overfit sooner rather than perform better — the data, not the architecture, is the binding constraint here.

Why train your own segmentation model rather than use a foundation model?

A general-purpose segmenter needs a prompt — a click or a box telling it what to cut out — and it is far too large to run in a browser. We need something that finds the wound unprompted, on the device, in about a second. A task-specific 12 MB U-Net does that. If the clinical question were interactive annotation rather than automatic triage, the answer would be different.

Why two binary heads instead of predicting the Texas cell directly?

Three reasons. The Texas grid has a dozen occupied cells, and splitting 800 images across them leaves too few per cell to fit or to evaluate. Depth and infection are conceptually independent, so forcing them into one label discards that structure. And the stage axis needs ischaemia, which no photograph contains, so a single combined output would be partly unidentifiable by construction. Two heads plus clinician input for perfusion is both more honest and easier to validate.

Is a Dice of 0.85 actually good?

It is in the range that published methods report on this dataset, and the median is 0.89. But the mean hides the tail: on sloughy wounds with indistinct edges it under-traces, and on one of my own examples it recovered only about a third of the true area. For serial measurement the failure mode matters more than the average, which is why the app shows the outline for the clinician to reject rather than reporting an area silently.

Why does it run in the browser rather than on a server, and why not federated learning?

In-browser inference means no image leaves the device, which removes the largest privacy and governance risk at source and makes the tool work when the clinic's internet does not. Federated learning solves a different problem — training across sites without pooling data — and it is premature here: it presumes several sites already collecting labelled data to an agreed protocol. We do not have one yet. Get the single-site reference standard right first.

How do you know the improvement came from cropping and not from chance?

Same architecture, same data, same initialisation, same schedule — only the input geometry changed, and both discrimination and accuracy moved together on both heads. I also learnt this lesson the hard way elsewhere: a single half-split suggested that calibrating the infection head improved accuracy, and averaging over twenty disjoint half-splits showed it made things worse. That is why the calibration is applied to one head and not the other, and why the reason is written into the shipped model file.

Who owns it?

The code is mine and openly available. The public photographs remain the property of the research groups that released them, and their licences forbid clinical use, which is one reason the deployed version must be trained on our own data.
